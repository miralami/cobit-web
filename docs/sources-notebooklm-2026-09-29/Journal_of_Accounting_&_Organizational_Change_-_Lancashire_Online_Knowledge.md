**Abstract **

**Purpose: The aim of the current study is two-fold. First, to investigate perceptions of audit **professionals regarding the challenges of implementing conventional remote auditing, with a comparison between Big4 and non-Big4 audit firms. Second, to explore the relevant challenges of utilizing artificial intelligence (AI) in remote IT auditing. **Methodology and Design: A questionnaire survey was conducted with 208 external audit **professionals to examine the challenges associated with traditional remote auditing. Given that AI-based remote IT auditing is an emerging practice, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 11 IT audit professionals to explore potential relevant challenges in its implementation. **Findings: Survey findings showed that creation a heavier workload for auditors and weak IT **infrastructure is most challenging issues for conventional remote auditing related to audit firms. Serious challenges pertain to audit clients are un-cooperation and resistance, and lack of expertise and IT infrastructure. These challenges were more pronounced for non-big4 in comparison to big4 counterparts. The identified challenges of using AI in remote IT auditing include lack of suitable AI infrastructure and compatibility with client systems, data quality and accessibility. Impediments also encompass talent gaps, resistance to change, and communication barriers. Privacy and cybersecurity concerns, along with the readiness of clients, are the common environmental challenges. **Originality: This study contributes originality by examining barriers to remote auditing in an **underexplored emerging market, comparing Big4 and non-Big4 firms. It also pioneers a qualitative investigation into challenges of adopting AI in remote IT auditing, addressing a critical gap identified in prior research. **Implications: The findings enhance the ongoing discussion regarding digital transformation in **auditing, especially for smaller audit firms in emer ing economies, where limited resources frequently impede technological progress. Audit firms and audit clients can be informed regarding the relevant common hurdles of different forms of remote auditing, and thus they can initiate organizational change by implementing practical strategies to overcome them. 

Keywords: Remote Auditing, AI, Remote IT Auditing, Innovation Challenges, External Audit, Big4 Firms.

**1. Introduction **

The transition to remote auditing has been traditionally enabled by developments in information and communication technology (ICT), allowing auditors to collect data, get electronic evidence, and conduct interviews with auditees utilizing different traditional ICT platforms. This has yielded many benefits for audit firms, such as cost savings, increased flexibility, and broader audit coverage (Lorentzon et al., 2024; Rabbani, 2024; Ovaska & Murphy, 2022; Sian, 2024). These ICT applications supporting conventional remote auditing comprise email, teleconferencing tools, secure data-sharing platforms, and real-time video streaming (Kljajić et al., 2022). Nevertheless, a more recent breakthrough is the emergence of remote auditing of IT systems through the use of AI technologies (Buhari, 2024). Leveraging AI in remote IT auditing is further transforming the traditional audit landscape by enabling auditors to perform efficient, real-time assessments without being physically present. AI techniques such as machine learning, natural language processing, and robotic process automation empower auditors to analyze vast datasets, detect anomalies, and conduct continuous monitoring, thereby enhancing audit accuracy and timeliness (Fedyk et al., 2022; Seethamraju & Hecimovic, 2023). These technologies offer significant benefits, including improved risk assessment, compliance verification, streamlined processes, reduced human error, and deeper analytical insights (Fedyk et al., 2022). 

Notwithstanding the extensively documented advantages of remote auditing—both conventional and modern, utilizing AI technologies—previous research has indicated that, due to various challenges, this practice is not yet widely developed, especially in smaller audit firms and emerging countries, including Jordan (Alma’aitah et al., 2024). In particular, these challenges are distinct and often more severe when related to the emerging practice of using AI for remote IT auditing (Buhari, 2024). This may be attributed to various reported challenges that hinder the effective implementation of these two forms of remote auditing. 

While the literature (e.g. Lois et al., 2020; Teeter et al., 2010; Ovaska & Murphy, 2022; Lorentzon et al., 2024) has fairly reported on the common challenges of implementing traditional remote auditing, which often comprise difficulties related to technical audit issues, resistance to change, and resources and technological limitations of both the auditee and the auditor, there remains a lack of such investigation in developing countr es like Jordan, particularly in comparing Big4 firms with non-Big4 audit firms, where technical, technological, and contextual challenges may significantly differ. Smaller firms, especially non-Big 4 entities, frequently encounter further challenges that impede the successful use of these approaches. The constraints encompass insufficient human and IT infrastructure, apprehensions regarding cybersecurity threats, reluctance towards technological transformation, and insufficient training in digital auditing instruments, among others (Lugli & Bertacchini, 2023; Manita et al., 2020; Li et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2025). Thus, the first part of this study strives to fill this gap in the literature by conducting a quantitative survey among audit professionals in Jordan into challenges of implanting remote auditing, comparing big4 and non-big4 firms. 

Literature also reported on the key obstacles to integrating AI technologies into auditing practice in general, which might include data quality issues, limited trust in AI systems due to their opaque decision-making processes, explainability of outcomes, privacy and security, absence of regulatory standards, and the need for organizational readiness and ethical oversight (Seethamraju & Hecimovic, 2023; Yang et al., 2021; Fedyk et al., 2022; 2024, Konika et al.,

Audit firms can collaborate with clients to offer support in the adoption of necessary technologies so that both parties benefit from a smoother remote auditing process. **1.1 Audit landscape in Jordan  **The audit environment in Jordan is characterized by a structured regulatory framework, a diverse landscape of audit firms, and an increasing engagement in providing services to the wider regional countries. The primary regulatory body overseeing the audit profession in Jordan is the Jordanian Association of Certified Public Accountants (JACPA), established in 1988 (Civil Society Organizations in Jordan, n.d.). JACPA is a mandatory membership organization for Certified Public Accountants, responsible for setting professional standards, regulating practice, and ensuring adherence to ethical guidelines, often aligning with international auditing standards (JACPA, n.d.). Jordan hosts a mix of audit firms, ranging from the local offices of the Big Four firms (PwC, Deloitte, EY, KPMG) to numerous national and international mid-tier and smaller firms. These firms provide a comprehensive array of audit and assurance services, tax advisory, and business consulting (e.g., PKF Jordan, ECOVIS Jordan, Crowe Jordan). While the Big Four firms typically cater to larger, often multinational clients, including those with operations across the region, smaller and mid-tier firms serve a significant portion of the local market. 

A notable trend in the Jordanian audit profession is the growing provision of audit services, which are often delivered remotely, to local and regional clients. This encompasses markets in countries such as Syria, Libya, Egypt, Iraq, and the GCC region, through the efficient utilization of current advanced remote audit technologies. Being a significant destination for audit and accounting services, the increasing business process digitalization, coupled with geographic proximity and regional affinity, position the Jordanian audit firms well to efficiently utilize remote audit technologies with their customers from these markets (Alma’aitah et al., 2024). This strategic approach involves the utilization of traditional ICT as well as increasingly benefiting from next-gen technologies such as AI and blockchain, thereby transforming the way audit work is conducted across borders (JACPA, n.d.). This movement into regional markets is generally spearheaded through the competitive landscape and the high degree of developed digital infrastructure, particularly within the GCC countries, allowing the Jordanian firms to increase their scope, diversify their customer base, and potentially position Jordan as the supporting hub for remote audit delivery to the entire MENA region. 

This regional status of the audit profession has fuelled the urgency of alternative audit mechanisms within Jordan, substantiating the value of determining and overcoming challenges inhibiting effective implementation of remote auditing practices (Alma’aitah et al., 2024). Hence, Jordan constitutes an interesting case study in exploring challenges of effective implementation of remote auditing, particularly wherein some challenges continue to exist for non-Big4 audit firms vulnerable to budgetary limitations and outdated technology, inhibiting their effective implementation of remote auditing. Focusing on Jordan, the study purports to yield results with relevance exceeding the national level, with practical applicability to other emerging markets experiencing comparable transformations.

**2.2.2 Challenges of using AI in remote IT auditing **Information Technology systems have become the backbone of modern organizations, underpinning essential business operations such as enterprise resource planning, accounting and finance, and supply chain management (Buhari, 2024). With the increasing reliance on these systems to handle critical and sensitive data, their reliability, performance, and security have become paramount concerns (Laudon, 2014). As a result, IT auditing has emerged as a crucial function, ensuring that systems operate effectively, comply with regulations, and support organizational objectives. Effective auditing can proactively identify system weaknesses, enhance usability, and contribute to continuous improvement (Hall, 2016). 

Traditionally, IT audits were managed through on-site testing, such as actual observation of hardware and verification of access controls, interviews with IT staff, and comprehensive reviews of system documentation and procedures (Stoltz, 2024). However, manual IT auditing, while effective in IT governance and identifying potential risks and threats, tended to take a lot of time and effort, hence making the audit cycle lengthy. Utilization of manual audits also increase the potential for human error, which may stretch the reliability of audit outputs (Buhari, 2024). Apart from that, they are subject to bias, and in some cases, information manipulation (Han et al., 2023). Hence, while organizations continue to increasingly depend on big-scale, integrated electronic infrastructures, traditional auditing's shortcomings become even more pronounced. Therefore, The increasing size, complexity and velocity of data-flow encountered in modern-day IT infrastructures present serious challenges to making traditional approaches viable (Buhari, 2024; Assidi et al., 2025). 

These challenges emphasize the importance of more efficient, accurate and scalable solutions, opening the way to the integration of advanced technologies such as AI in remote IT auditing. AI, with the support of blockchain and cloud computing, achieves continuous auditing through timely monitoring of transactions and systems, reducing the time lag between generation of data and audit intervention. This shift from periodic and manual checks to continuous analysis augments responsiveness and diminishes the possibility of undetected anomalies (Cha et al., 2023). Moreover, AI systems can self-improve over time, refining their models to suit different organizational contexts and emerging threats, introducing an aspect of scalability, speed and agility in responsiveness, which human audits unable to match (Fedyk et al., 2022; Vitali and Giuliani, 2024). AI also enables audit depth through the analysis of large, complex data sets, and enhances the detection of irregularities and anomalies possibly going undetected with human methods (Stoltz, 2024); making it effective in spotting risks and non-compliance issues. It does also mundane work automatically, introduces consistency, and allows shifting focus away from the auditors on more strategic tasks such as analysis and decision-making (Regueiro et al., 2021). 

While promising, effective integration of AI in IT audit, specifically in remote settings, presents new challenges inherent in such a technological innovation. The TOE framework provides a lens through which to investigate factors underlying the adoption and diffusion of new emergent technological innovations, including AI application in remote audit. This framework brings to the forefront technological, organizational, and environmental factors. Existing research (e.g., Murikah et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2021; Manita et al., 2020; Fedyk et al., 2022; Seethamraju and Hecimovic, 2023; Konika et al., 2025) examine predictors commonly influencing AI adoption in audit, including the need for technological infrastructure, explainability and transparency of algorithms, ethical considerations, regulatory needs, and skilled human staff capable enough to interpret AI outputs. Technological limitations present a prime area of focus, considering that many organizations, especially smaller ones, lack the required infrastructure to execute remote IT audit work with efficiency. AI technologies also present the challenge of requiring high-quality data to function effectively, with poor-quality data having the potential to yield inaccurate or unreliable audit findings (Seethamraju and Hecimovic, 2023; Konika et al., 2025).

**3. Methodology and Methods **Since the aim of this study is two-fold, two distinct methods of data collection and analysis were employed as appropriate: a questionnaire survey and semi-structured interviews. As traditional remote auditing is a relatively well-researched area, the questionnaire survey was used to collect quantitative data from audit professionals to examine its challenges, with a comparative focus on Big4 and non-Big4 audit firms. In contrast, the interview method was adopted to qualitatively explore the challenges of using AI in remote IT auditing, given that it is a newly emerging area with limited prior research. However, numerous challenges exist, especially for smaller audit firms. Overcoming these challenges requires specific strategies to facilitate the sustainable and effective adoption of remote auditing procedures. Accordingly, this paper explores an important area where previous literature has had a major gap. It first quantitatively analyzes the challenges that audit firms in Jordan face in implementing traditional remote auditing, with a particular focus on comparing Big4 and non-Big4 firms. The survey results reveal that auditors consistently report more-than-average level of challenges pertaining to remote auditing. The challenges of remote IT auditing identified by interview participants relevant to technology include: lack of suitable AI infrastructure and compatibility with client systems, data quality and accessibility. Organizationally, impediments encompass talent gaps, resistance to change, and communication barriers. Privacy and cybersecurity concerns, along with the readiness and cooperation of clients, are the common environmental challenges.

**5.2 Contributions and Implications **

The findings of this study offer substantial contributions and have significant implications for audit firms, audit clients, and policymakers, especially within emerging economies. By incorporating practitioner evidence with a stakeholder perspective, the study offers many contributions including insights for practitioners regarding operational obstacles and capability requirements; guidance for clients on readiness, data governance, and change management to facilitate effective engagement in remote/AI-enabled audits; and implications for regulators concerning standards, guidance, and assurance related to AI utilisation. 

The study presents empirical facts concerning the principal issues encountered by audit firms in Jordan related to remote auditing, emphasising the digital disparity between Big4 and non-Big4 organisations. Practical insights indicate that although remote auditing provides efficiency improvements, it concurrently elevates auditors' workloads, hence requiring the implementation of automation tools, workflow optimisation, and AI-enhanced audit techniques to boost productivity. For audit clients, outcomes manifest as a preparedness framework for explicitly delineated data-access and logging protocols; established minimal data-quality standards for AI analytics; appointed client-side representatives for technical enquiries; developed systematic communication frameworks to mitigate misunderstandings in remote environments. 

The current study highlights the deficiency of critical IT infrastructure, especially inside non-Big4 organisations, underscoring the necessity for strategic investments in cloud platforms, cybersecurity protocols, and real-time data-sharing technologies. These findings have significant policy consequences, as regulatory agencies can effectively facilitate digital transformation through financial incentives, training programs, and technology subsidies. Therefore, regulators can improve audit quality and market confidence by providing guidance and safe-harbour provisions regarding many aspects comprising acceptable remote access practices and evidence adequacy when utilising AI tools; documentation standards for AI-assisted procedures (including model transparency, data provenance, and bias/robustness assessments); and minimum control requirements for client environments. 

The current study identifies major barriers to performing specific audit operations remotely, recommending hybrid audit models that combine in-person verification with remote techniques while utilising data analytics and blockchain to improve audit dependability. Realising that non-Big4 enterprises encounter significant hurdles, specialised support programs are crucial to address resource deficiencies and promote equitable digital adoption throughout the industry. This study highlights the significance of strategic investments in digital infrastructure, enhanced auditor-client collaboration, and proactive policy measures to bridge the digital divide with larger firms, to create a competitive advantage, as well as elevate audit quality, efficiency, and resilience in a progressively digital audit landscape.
