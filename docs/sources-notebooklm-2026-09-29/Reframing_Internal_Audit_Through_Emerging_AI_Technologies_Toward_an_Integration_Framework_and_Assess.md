## Abstract

The rapid adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies into organizational information systems is reshaping internal audit practices and governance mechanisms. Nevertheless, notable gaps persist in both the academic literature and professional practice in integrating AI technologies within the internal audit function and in assessing its maturity level. To address these limitations, this study employs a two-stage methodological approach. First, a Systematic Literature Review (SLR) was conducted following PRISMA 2020 and PRISMA-S guidelines to synthesize existing knowledge and identify structural gaps in AI integration within the internal audit function (IAF). Second, drawing on the SLR findings, the study follows a theory-building and conceptual artifact-design approach. Three instruments are developed and assessed: the Internal Audit–Artificial Intelligence Integration Framework (IA-AIIF), the Internal Audit–AI Integration Assessment Cube (IA-AI Cube), and a Hierarchical Weighted Scoring Model (HWSM). These instruments enable the multidimensional evaluation of AI integration across technical, operational, and governance dimensions. They may offer guidance for both practitioners and researchers advancing AI-enabled approaches within the IAF. The findings suggest practical, managerial, and theoretical contributions by supporting AI integration in internal audit practice, outlining implementation-oriented recommendations for AI adoption, and advancing the body of knowledge at the intersection of internal audit and emerging AI technologies. ## 1. Introduction

The internal audit (IA) profession is undergoing notable change, driven by Artificial Intelligence (AI) and advanced Machine Learning (ML), including Deep Learning (DL) for pattern recognition and Reinforcement Learning (RL) for autonomous decision-making. Traditionally known for retrospective testing and manual sampling, internal auditing is transforming into a technology-driven function capable of delivering continuous, predictive insights. The recent literature emphasizes that AI is not merely a tool for incremental improvement but a driver that is redefining how audits are planned, executed, and reported [[1](https://www.mdpi.com#B1-electronics-15-02280),[2](https://www.mdpi.com#B2-electronics-15-02280),[3](https://www.mdpi.com#B3-electronics-15-02280),[4](https://www.mdpi.com#B4-electronics-15-02280)]. Current AI adoption rates in organizations’ internal audit functions vary. For instance, research indicates that while 80% of internal auditors recognize the importance of AI, only about 37% are actively utilizing these technologies [[5](https://www.mdpi.com#B5-electronics-15-02280)]. This gap underscores the need for a more comprehensive understanding of the strategic, organizational, and technical factors involved in integrating AI into internal audit processes [[6](https://www.mdpi.com#B6-electronics-15-02280),[7](https://www.mdpi.com#B7-electronics-15-02280)]. According to a recent report by Wolters Kluwer [[8](https://www.mdpi.com#B8-electronics-15-02280)], the use of AI in internal auditing is expected to reach 80% by the end of 2026, effectively doubling current adoption rates. To support this transition, the industry is prioritizing the implementation of dedicated internal audit AI technologies. Ultimately, the report highlights how this rapid technological integration is transforming the field into a more accurate and future-ready profession. In line with these endeavors, our paper primarily aims to examine how AI integration may enhance internal audit practices to increase efficiency, accuracy, and strategic value.

### 1.1. Conceptual Context and Research Scope

Research indicates that there are notably more studies on AI in financial auditing than in internal auditing. Unlike financial auditing, which focuses on the accuracy of financial statements [[9](https://www.mdpi.com#B9-electronics-15-02280)], internal auditing encompasses a broader mandate: evaluating risk management, governance, and operational controls [[10](https://www.mdpi.com#B10-electronics-15-02280)]. This distinction is critical for defining the research scope clearly and avoiding conceptual overlap between two distinct mechanisms, as AI integration poses unique challenges for the internal audit function. As defined by The Institute of Internal Auditors (IIA), “internal auditing strengthens the organization’s ability to create, protect, and sustain value by providing the board and management with independent, risk-based, and objective assurance, advice, insight, and foresight” [[11](https://www.mdpi.com#B11-electronics-15-02280)]. Internal audits are integrated into organizational control and quality systems. They support continuous monitoring and improvement and apply analytical procedures across traditional and emerging digital environments [[12](https://www.mdpi.com#B12-electronics-15-02280)]. Internal audits function independently but remain a separate part of the organization’s structure. They are carried out by employees or co-sourced professionals under the supervision of management and the audit committee [[10](https://www.mdpi.com#B10-electronics-15-02280)]. In contrast, a financial audit is conducted by external firms to provide statutory assurance to external stakeholders [[9](https://www.mdpi.com#B9-electronics-15-02280),[12](https://www.mdpi.com#B12-electronics-15-02280)]. The aforementioned considerations highlight that the internal audit function extends well beyond traditional financial controls. It requires assessing complex, heterogeneous, and often unstructured operational data. As a central pillar of governance, the internal audit function (IAF), supported by a robust Internal Control System (ICS), is essential for validating Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) data and non-financial reporting before they reach external auditors. By offering both assurance and strategic foresight, the ICS helps organizations meet stakeholder expectations and improve their overall sustainability impact. Experts in the field view internal auditing as a key mechanism for organizational control, primarily developed as a regulatory response to significant 20th-century financial scandals [[13](https://www.mdpi.com#B13-electronics-15-02280)]. In practice, IA is structured to inform internal policies and optimize operational activities. Consequently, IA serves as the foundation for the most compelling arguments in favor of robust governance and corporate accountability reform [[14](https://www.mdpi.com#B14-electronics-15-02280)]. As practitioners face intensifying pressure to optimize resource allocation while simultaneously enhancing internal audit efficiency, the integration of AI technologies within IAF has become increasingly critical [[15](https://www.mdpi.com#B15-electronics-15-02280)]. Scholars have already reported that aggregating diverse data sources increases the efficiency of the IA planning phase [[16](https://www.mdpi.com#B16-electronics-15-02280)]. In the contemporary digital landscape, AI has emerged, advancing solutions that simulate human cognitive processes to perform complex tasks previously reserved for the human intellect [[17](https://www.mdpi.com#B17-electronics-15-02280)]. Within this context, our investigation focused on integrating emerging AI technologies into the organization’s IT infrastructure to support internal auditors’ tasks.

### 1.2. Aligning Literature Gaps with Research Questions and Objectives

Despite the increasing emphasis on AI in internal auditing, research on the adoption, operationalization, governance, and impact of emerging AI technologies within IAF remains limited and fragmented. Scholars report a scarcity of studies and conceptual frameworks that explicitly address advanced AI technologies in internal audit contexts [[18](https://www.mdpi.com#B18-electronics-15-02280),[19](https://www.mdpi.com#B19-electronics-15-02280)]. Additionally, Ref. [[20](https://www.mdpi.com#B20-electronics-15-02280)] observes that the integration of AI into internal auditing remains largely unexplored. Studies on AI adoption in accounting and auditing contexts indicate that barriers, including transparency, explainability, and regulatory uncertainty, hinder the integration of advanced AI; they suggest similar or greater challenges for the adoption of Generative AI (GenAI) in internal auditing [[21](https://www.mdpi.com#B21-electronics-15-02280),[22](https://www.mdpi.com#B22-electronics-15-02280)]. Evidence from audit quality research indicates that AI tools can enhance accuracy and efficiency; however, the specific effects on internal audit quality outcomes and assurance processes remain unclear [[23](https://www.mdpi.com#B23-electronics-15-02280)]. Ref. [[24](https://www.mdpi.com#B24-electronics-15-02280)] also highlights a critical misalignment between the accelerated deployment of AI and the development of dedicated internal audit frameworks. Reviews focused on ethics and governance in AI auditing identify substantial heterogeneity in ethical principles and stakeholder concerns, indicating that governance frameworks for responsible GenAI use in audit practices are still underdeveloped [[25](https://www.mdpi.com#B25-electronics-15-02280),[26](https://www.mdpi.com#B26-electronics-15-02280)]. Despite the availability of technical implementation standards issued by the IIA, these frameworks remain insufficient to fully address the broader governance and control of IAF requirements. None of these studies addresses either the operationalization of emerging AI adoption in internal audit processes or the dynamic assessment of the maturity level of integrating such technological solutions. Competency research in accounting and auditing points to a need for structured skill development and organizational integration strategies to support effective AI implementation, yet these are not well articulated in AI contexts [[27](https://www.mdpi.com#B27-electronics-15-02280)]. Ref. [[28](https://www.mdpi.com#B28-electronics-15-02280)] examines implementation readiness for digital internal audit adoption in the Malaysian public sector, identifying relationships between change commitment, change valence, task knowledge, task availability, and change efficacy. However, the study does not address AI integration within the IAF. Ref. [[29](https://www.mdpi.com#B29-electronics-15-02280)] explicitly highlights the need for AI governance frameworks that balance security, compliance, and innovation. The authors conclude that overly restrictive policies may deter misuse but may also encourage Shadow AI, thereby increasing exposure to unmonitored risks. Collectively, the identified gaps highlight the need to clarify how emerging AI technologies are integrated, governed, and evaluated within internal audit practice. These gaps guide the formulation of focused inquiries into the integration and maturity level assessment of AI within the internal audit function. Accordingly, the study addresses the following research questions (RQ): RQ1: What key structural components should a framework include to effectively integrate emerging AI technologies into an organization’s information system for improving internal audit outcomes? RQ2: How could the integration of emerging AI technologies be quantified within an organization’s internal audit function? Considering the gaps outlined above and in response to the research questions, this study adopts a two-stage methodological approach. In the first stage, a systematic literature review (SLR) was conducted to synthesize the existing body of knowledge and identify structural gaps in how emerging AI technologies are integrated and evaluated within the IAF. In the second stage, the SLR findings inform a theory-building and conceptual artifact-design process [[30](https://www.mdpi.com#B30-electronics-15-02280),[31](https://www.mdpi.com#B31-electronics-15-02280)]. This process yields three original conceptual instruments: the IA-AIIF (Internal Audit–Artificial Intelligence Integration Framework), a complementary evaluation model comprising the Internal Audit–AI Integration Assessment Cube (IA-AI Cube), and a Hierarchical Weighted Scoring Model (HWSM). The study follows an established approach to conceptual research, in which framework development and model construction constitute recognized scientific contributions that precede empirical validation. This perspective is consistent with theory-building traditions in information systems research [[32](https://www.mdpi.com#B32-electronics-15-02280)], where artifact design and analytical validation represent a necessary stage prior to field-based testing. The logical coherence and preliminary operational feasibility of the proposed instruments are assessed through complementary validation approaches. For the IA-AIIF, the assessment draws on trace validation from Sargent’s simulation validation taxonomy [[33](https://www.mdpi.com#B33-electronics-15-02280)]. For the HWSM and IA-AI Cube, validation is conducted through simulation-based sensitivity analysis, which examines the model’s responsiveness to strategic weight reallocation across dimensions and comparative evaluation against a Conventional Equal-Weight Model (CEWM), which confirms the discriminatory advantage of the hierarchical weighting approach. Together, these techniques constitute a multi-method validation approach appropriate for the proposed instruments at this stage of development. This paper proceeds with a review of the relevant literature and a presentation of the research methodology. It then presents and discusses the findings in relation to the research questions, concluding with the study’s contributions, limitations, and directions for future research.

## 2. Consolidated Insights from the Literature Review

### 2.1. The Limitations of Traditional Internal Audits

An internal audit provides independent and objective assurance that an organization’s risk management, control, and governance processes are operating effectively [[34](https://www.mdpi.com#B34-electronics-15-02280)]. The literature identifies several critical inefficiencies in the traditional IAF: historically, auditing methods have been characterized by repetitive manual tasks prone to human error and high levels of uncertainty [[35](https://www.mdpi.com#B35-electronics-15-02280),[36](https://www.mdpi.com#B36-electronics-15-02280),[37](https://www.mdpi.com#B37-electronics-15-02280)]. Ref. [[35](https://www.mdpi.com#B35-electronics-15-02280)] emphasizes that the function should move away from manual procedures toward more systematic and comprehensive predictive models. Another major point of concern is the reliance on representative sampling, where auditors typically select a subset of transactions for evaluation [[38](https://www.mdpi.com#B38-electronics-15-02280)]. Similarly, in internal audit engagements, drawing sample transactions and comparing them to guidelines remains a key part of collecting and assessing information [[39](https://www.mdpi.com#B39-electronics-15-02280)]. This study points out that these samples often represent less than 1% of the total population, leading to critical information and errors being frequently missed. When performed manually, the internal audit relies on consulting past records and conducting face-to-face document analysis, which slows down project execution [[40](https://www.mdpi.com#B40-electronics-15-02280)]. The authors explain that a non-conformity missed in a traditional sample could propagate its effects throughout organizational processes before the next audit cycle. The literature review highlights the need to modernize internal audit operational practices by integrating emerging AI technologies.

### 2.2. Artificial Intelligence in Internal Audit—A Changing Landscape

Internal auditing should evolve from relying on “sample-dependent and compliance audits” to adopting more sophisticated, predictive models [[35](https://www.mdpi.com#B35-electronics-15-02280)]. This transformation moves away from traditional sampling toward a data-driven model in which automated processes enable faster, more complex, and more pervasive evaluations of organizational health [[38](https://www.mdpi.com#B38-electronics-15-02280)]. Additionally, Ref. [[36](https://www.mdpi.com#B36-electronics-15-02280)] suggests that AI-enhanced processes not only eliminate manual procedures but also substantially improve the quality, speed, and reliability of IA findings. Integrating AI into the organizational environment requires a fundamental rethinking of the IAF, evolving to a continuous, strategic, and predictive one [[18](https://www.mdpi.com#B18-electronics-15-02280),[41](https://www.mdpi.com#B41-electronics-15-02280),[42](https://www.mdpi.com#B42-electronics-15-02280)]. The emergence of AI is enabling auditors to move toward intelligent learning systems [[43](https://www.mdpi.com#B43-electronics-15-02280),[44](https://www.mdpi.com#B44-electronics-15-02280),[45](https://www.mdpi.com#B45-electronics-15-02280)]. The current era is defined by the “scaling hypothesis” postulating that increasing the size of training datasets, the number of parameters, and the amount of computation leads to a predictable reduction in test loss, representing a power law of performance improvement [[34](https://www.mdpi.com#B34-electronics-15-02280)]. A benefit emphasized in the literature is the enhancement of human decision-making in internal audits through AI. Ref. [[35](https://www.mdpi.com#B35-electronics-15-02280)] advocates a comprehensive adoption of AI to improve accuracy, efficiency, strategic oversight, and risk assessment. AI provides a significant set of capabilities to increase the accuracy, productivity, and predictive capacity of the internal audit [[23](https://www.mdpi.com#B23-electronics-15-02280)]. Its integration without an adequate conceptual framework can generate major hazards, such as algorithmic bias, confabulation, lack of transparency (“black box”), data quality issues, over-reliance on AI, cybersecurity vulnerabilities, accountability dilution, and regulatory and compliance risks [[18](https://www.mdpi.com#B18-electronics-15-02280),[46](https://www.mdpi.com#B46-electronics-15-02280),[47](https://www.mdpi.com#B47-electronics-15-02280)]. Ref. [[48](https://www.mdpi.com#B48-electronics-15-02280)] underscores the imperative for ethically grounded AI governance, encompassing integrated risk management, transparent communication, stakeholder engagement, robust accountability mechanisms, and continuous oversight. The risks are exacerbated when AI implementation occurs in the absence of structured conceptual frameworks, clearly defined governance mechanisms, or standardized control structures [[23](https://www.mdpi.com#B23-electronics-15-02280),[47](https://www.mdpi.com#B47-electronics-15-02280)]. Recent studies [[18](https://www.mdpi.com#B18-electronics-15-02280),[46](https://www.mdpi.com#B46-electronics-15-02280)] point out the shortage of comprehensive frameworks for effectively using AI in the IAF. This situation indicates that organizations lack clear guidelines and a coherent approach to integrating AI technologies into internal audit processes, leading to fragmented, inefficient implementations [[49](https://www.mdpi.com#B49-electronics-15-02280)]. Some papers focus on the skills and training required for an internal auditor to work effectively in an AI auditing environment. As such, Refs. [[38](https://www.mdpi.com#B38-electronics-15-02280),[50](https://www.mdpi.com#B50-electronics-15-02280)] emphasize that internal auditors need to enhance their skills in analyzing business processes and identifying related risks and controls. Ref. [[51](https://www.mdpi.com#B51-electronics-15-02280)] also notes that IT knowledge and critical thinking skills are now essential for the practice. Ref. [[52](https://www.mdpi.com#B52-electronics-15-02280)] finds that the positive effect of AI on internal audit quality is more salient when the auditor has an IT background. Several studies emphasize that AI is intended to augment rather than replace auditors, as human professionals remain essential for exercising creativity, professional skepticism, expertise, and value-based judgment [[35](https://www.mdpi.com#B35-electronics-15-02280),[36](https://www.mdpi.com#B36-electronics-15-02280),[53](https://www.mdpi.com#B53-electronics-15-02280),[54](https://www.mdpi.com#B54-electronics-15-02280)]. Ref. [[55](https://www.mdpi.com#B55-electronics-15-02280)] sees internal auditors as “gatekeepers” who ensure accountability for information and safeguard shareholders’ wealth in a data-driven world. They highlight that the “human factor” remains vital in decision-making. As these studies suggest, in the changing landscape of internal audit, integrating emerging AI technologies requires developing an appropriate organizational context and providing the necessary tools and competencies to support them.

### 2.3. The “Copiloted” Auditing Model

The integration of AI technology into the IAF has been the subject of discussions regarding the extent of AI’s involvement in internal audit processes. Ref. [[56](https://www.mdpi.com#B56-electronics-15-02280)] views the integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) like ChatGPT (GPT-4) as a “copiloted” auditing system. In this model, the auditor manages the process and procedures, with AI acting as an “audit-focused assistant” that provides insights and analysis to support informed decision-making. Ref. [[39](https://www.mdpi.com#B39-electronics-15-02280)] highlights that process knowledge is ultimately necessary for the auditor to validate the anomalies detected by AI and to derive the final conclusions for the audit report. The above findings indicate that tools such as LLMs and GenAI cannot be excluded from systems supporting internal audit procedures. Consequently, their integration should be considered alongside appropriate measures to mitigate the risks associated with these emerging technologies.

### 2.4. Emerging Artificial Intelligence Technologies for the Internal Audit Function

In November 2022, ChatGPT marked a new phase in AI development, transitioning to GenAI. Between 2023 and 2025, LLMs evolved rapidly, significantly improving reasoning, context understanding, multilingual performance, and multimodal capabilities. This period also introduced Agentic AI systems, which can plan, execute multi-step tasks, use external tools, and operate with greater autonomy. The development of AI is now experiencing a shift from static LLMs to dynamic, tool-enabled Agentic AI. Ref. [[57](https://www.mdpi.com#B57-electronics-15-02280)] makes a rigorous distinction between Machine Learning (ML) and Agentic AI. While ML is often characterized as a passive predictive model, Agentic AI is defined as a dynamic, interactive system capable of sensing conditions in its environment. It can execute goal-driven actions to influence specific outcomes and operate proactively rather than merely respond to static user prompts. While early generative models were primarily valued for their ability to produce human-like text in isolation, enterprise-grade automation requires systems that can reason, access real-time data, and execute actions within complex environments. This is a transition from AI as a chatbot to AI as an autonomous collaborator capable of iterative refinement and goal-oriented behavior.

 2.4.1. Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG)

Retrieval Augmented Generation, introduced by [[58](https://www.mdpi.com#B58-electronics-15-02280)], represents a significant advancement in knowledge-grounded AI systems. RAG combines (1) a retrieval component, responsible for identifying relevant documents from an external knowledge base; (2) a generation component, which produces responses conditioned on the retrieved context. This architecture allows AI systems to generate outputs that are both context-aware and verifiable, addressing the limitations of purely parametric models. RAG is defined in the recent literature as an integrative framework that couples LLMs with external information retrieval systems to mitigate the risks of “knowledge staleness” and hallucinations [[57](https://www.mdpi.com#B57-electronics-15-02280),[59](https://www.mdpi.com#B59-electronics-15-02280),[60](https://www.mdpi.com#B60-electronics-15-02280)]. By conditioning the generation process on authoritative, domain-specific passages retrieved during inference, RAG ensures verifiable sources and enhances credibility in technical contexts or numerical claims with trustworthy references [[59](https://www.mdpi.com#B59-electronics-15-02280)]. The primary contribution of RAG in specialized fields (like audits, healthcare, or engineering) is its mechanism for factual grounding [[61](https://www.mdpi.com#B61-electronics-15-02280)]. By grounding the model’s outputs in audited documents, regulatory codes, or specialized literature, RAG offers auditable provenance [[59](https://www.mdpi.com#B59-electronics-15-02280)]. Recent evolutions of the concept include Self-Correcting Iterative Mechanisms (SCIMs), which transform RAG from a passive “one-shot” mechanism into an active reasoning architecture [[62](https://www.mdpi.com#B62-electronics-15-02280)].

 2.4.2. Vector Databases

In the RAG paradigm, Vector Databases (VecDBs) act as an external, non-parametric memory for LLMs [[58](https://www.mdpi.com#B58-electronics-15-02280)]. Unlike traditional databases that store exact values, VecDBs store the semantic features of data. A VecDB is a specialized data management system designed to efficiently store, index, and retrieve high-dimensional data points known as vectors. These vectors are formed by vector embeddings, which are fixed-length numerical representations of unstructured data (such as text, images, or audio) generated by deep learning models [[63](https://www.mdpi.com#B63-electronics-15-02280)]. The distance between vectors in high-dimensional space represents their semantic similarity [[64](https://www.mdpi.com#B64-electronics-15-02280)]. To avoid the computational cost of brute-force exhaustive searches, VecDBs use specialized indexing techniques for Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) searches [[63](https://www.mdpi.com#B63-electronics-15-02280)]. In the RAG workflow, a VecDB is an important component that is ensures (1) ingestion—unstructured data are chunked, converted into embeddings, and stored in the VecDB; (2) retrieval—a user query is vectorized, and the VecDB retrieves the most relevant document fragments (Top-K results); and (3) generation—the retrieved context is appended to the original prompt, allowing the LLM to generate an answer anchored in verifiable evidence [[64](https://www.mdpi.com#B64-electronics-15-02280),[65](https://www.mdpi.com#B65-electronics-15-02280)]. The techniques and data-retrieval methods associated with VecDBs enable scalable semantic retrieval over large, heterogeneous repositories by avoiding computationally expensive brute-force searches and supporting similarity-based indexing [[66](https://www.mdpi.com#B66-electronics-15-02280)]. Ref. [[67](https://www.mdpi.com#B67-electronics-15-02280)] states that “traditional relational databases” cannot effectively handle descriptors represented as high-dimensional vectors. Therefore, they need specialized ML techniques, such as similarity searches. This VecDB capability is particularly relevant in internal audit environments characterized by massive organizational data lakes that contain both structured and unstructured data [[68](https://www.mdpi.com#B68-electronics-15-02280)]. By bypassing the processing bottlenecks of brute-force methods, auditing systems can rapidly extract metadata and identify anomalies across diverse sources, thereby providing more comprehensive assurance [[69](https://www.mdpi.com#B69-electronics-15-02280)]. In continuous auditing scenarios, this is extremely valuable, as timely access to relevant data is critical for anomaly detection, compliance verification, and risk assessment.

 2.4.3. Agentic AI

Agentic AI represents a major step forward in AI, moving from passive, predictive models to autonomous systems capable of acting to pursue goals. Unlike traditional or generative models that follow rigid instructions, these systems utilize reinforcement learning and adaptive control mechanisms to navigate unpredictable, real-world environments [[70](https://www.mdpi.com#B70-electronics-15-02280)]. Furthermore, Ref. [[57](https://www.mdpi.com#B57-electronics-15-02280)] states that in a multi-agent system, the Agentic AI functions as the “Central Coordinator”, breaking complex goals into multi-stage tasks without human oversight. In this sense, the system moves beyond single-pass pipelines to a model where decision, decomposition, router, and retrieval agents coordinate simultaneous searches and workflows. In the same line of thought, Ref. [[60](https://www.mdpi.com#B60-electronics-15-02280)] characterizes Agentic AI as a distributed, protocol-governed environment of specialized agents capable of coordinating complex tasks while maintaining factual provenance. To manage the risks of GenAI in multi-agent environments, Ref. [[71](https://www.mdpi.com#B71-electronics-15-02280)] proposes a “Sentinel Agent” architecture. These specialized AI entities monitor conversational flows in real time to detect malicious intent, sanitize inputs, and block exfiltration attempts. Thus, it can serve as a distributed security layer for the audit function.

 2.4.4. Model Context Protocol (MCP)

The Model Context Protocol is an open standard introduced by Anthropic in late 2024, designed to define how LLMs communicate with external applications, data sources, and tools [[72](https://www.mdpi.com#B72-electronics-15-02280)]. MCP harmonizes the interface between LLMs and external data sources or tools, as it functions as a “universal socket” [[57](https://www.mdpi.com#B57-electronics-15-02280)]. Leading AI companies and major platforms have integrated MCP to support multi-step task execution, transforming AI from passive text generation to active execution of operational tasks. Recent studies [[72](https://www.mdpi.com#B72-electronics-15-02280),[73](https://www.mdpi.com#B73-electronics-15-02280),[74](https://www.mdpi.com#B74-electronics-15-02280)] conceptualize the MCP as an architecture that decouples AI applications from underlying data sources and external services. This architecture consists of two components: MCP Host and MCP Client. The MCP Host represents the user-facing application responsible for orchestrating interactions and managing user consent. The MCP Client is an intermediary component within the host that maintains isolated connections to external systems. It translates communication between the language model and these systems. At the operational level, the MCP Server provides capabilities by exposing three core elements: tools (executable functions), resources (structured or unstructured data such as logs or files), and prompts (reusable templates). Together, these components enable standardized, modular, and controlled interaction between AI systems and enterprise data environments.

### 2.5. Professional Standards and Governance Frameworks: A Gray Literature Perspective

The investigation of frameworks, standards, practice guides, and reports of globally recognized governance and professional bodies—the Institute of Internal Auditors (IIA), the Committee of Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway Commission (COSO), the European Parliament and Council of the European Union, ISACA, and the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)—reveals a list of limitations as well as structural and operational gaps. The research team’s review of selected gray literature (GL) highlights a notable lack of comprehensive, practically applicable frameworks for integrating emerging AI technologies into internal audit practices. Although the analyzed frameworks [[11](https://www.mdpi.com#B11-electronics-15-02280),[75](https://www.mdpi.com#B75-electronics-15-02280),[76](https://www.mdpi.com#B76-electronics-15-02280)] demonstrate a high level of complementarity and broad applicability, to the best of our knowledge, there is currently no standardized framework specifically dedicated to the integration of emerging AI technologies within the IAF. The substantiation of these frameworks [[75](https://www.mdpi.com#B75-electronics-15-02280),[76](https://www.mdpi.com#B76-electronics-15-02280)] on broad, high-level principles—such as fairness, transparency, and accountability—complicates their translation into clear implementation methodologies for the use of emerging AI technologies within internal auditing. Although the IIA standards [[11](https://www.mdpi.com#B11-electronics-15-02280)] highlight the need for internal audit professionals to possess technological competencies, detailed competency frameworks specific to AI have yet to be developed. In the IIA’s global practice guide [[77](https://www.mdpi.com#B77-electronics-15-02280)], data analysis and information technologies are listed generically under the Knowledge and Skill Subcategories among the competencies required for an internal auditor. Nonetheless, both the academic literature and practice confirm a competency and skills gap. According to Optro’s 2026 annual Focus on the Future survey [[78](https://www.mdpi.com#B78-electronics-15-02280)], only 28% of leaders express confidence in their teams’ ability to audit AI risks. The survey shows a gap between aspirations and reality; an internal audit aims to be strategic, innovative, and valuable, but external perceptions, like limitations and traditionalism, stay the same. Another significant gap concerns governance and ownership. This is due to unclear roles, responsibilities, and decision-making authority in integrating AI tools within the IAF. The analyzed frameworks lack clear guidelines on accountability for AI use in internal audits. They do not specify who is responsible for validating AI-generated outputs or how roles should be divided among internal auditors, IT specialists, and AI systems [[75](https://www.mdpi.com#B75-electronics-15-02280)]. The limited focus on specific application areas creates additional shortcomings, including a lack of well-defined metrics, practical tools, and AI-specific guidance tailored to the IAF’s needs. A recurring theme in organizational reports is the structural gap between technological innovation and governance frameworks. The rapid pace of technological progress outstrips the capabilities of existing traditional governance models. This mismatch, especially evident in AI/GenAI systems, is reflected in what is often called regulatory inertia, indicating organizations’ limited capacity to keep up with evolving technologies [[79](https://www.mdpi.com#B79-electronics-15-02280)].

## 3. Research Methodology

To address the research questions and the gaps identified in the literature, a suitable methodological approach was adopted. Consequently, the study uses a two-stage methodological design. The first stage comprises a systematic literature review (SLR) undertaken to synthesize the existing body of knowledge and identify potential limitations associated with the integration of emerging AI technologies within the IAF. The second stage employs a theory-building and conceptual artifact-design approach to leverage the SLR results in developing the study outcomes. Consistent with this methodological orientation, the development of frameworks and models is regarded as a legitimate scholarly contribution that may precede empirical investigation.

### 3.1. The Systematic Literature Review Stage

A systematic literature review (SLR) was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 [[80](https://www.mdpi.com#B80-electronics-15-02280)] reporting guidelines. This provides a structured framework for documenting study identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and inclusion processes. To ensure full transparency and reproducibility of the literature search, the review additionally adhered to the PRISMA-S extension [[81](https://www.mdpi.com#B81-electronics-15-02280)], which requires detailed reporting of database platforms, search strategies, dates, applied limits, and supplementary methods such as gray literature searches.

 3.1.1. Information Sources and Methods

The literature search drew on Web of Science (WoS), selected for its curated multidisciplinary citation metadata, which support rigorous synthesis [[82](https://www.mdpi.com#B82-electronics-15-02280),[83](https://www.mdpi.com#B83-electronics-15-02280)]. After reviewing WoS-indexed papers, we acknowledged limited peer-reviewed evidence on integrating AI in internal audits. This finding is also supported by [[18](https://www.mdpi.com#B18-electronics-15-02280)], highlighting substantial gaps and few studies in WoS-indexed papers addressing adoption, theoretical frameworks, or practical implications. Subsequently, incorporating gray literature (GL) into systematic searches proved methodologically essential. To capture AI adoption in internal audits, given that industry reports precede peer-reviewed publications, we integrated GL with WoS sources to ensure comprehensive, timely evidence while mitigating publication bias [[84](https://www.mdpi.com#B84-electronics-15-02280),[85](https://www.mdpi.com#B85-electronics-15-02280)]. The workflow and search results are documented in a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram ([Figure 1](https://www.mdpi.com#electronics-15-02280-f001)), supplemented with details of search strategies per PRISMA-S guidance to enhance transparency and reproducibility. This dual application of PRISMA 2020 and PRISMA-S ensures a robust methodology for the systematic review, enabling clear reporting of the identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion of studies from both WoS-indexed and GL sources [[80](https://www.mdpi.com#B80-electronics-15-02280),[81](https://www.mdpi.com#B81-electronics-15-02280)]. The literature review was conducted between 12 January 2026 and 3 April 2026.

### 3.2. The Theory-Building and Conceptual Artifact-Design Stage

The findings of the SLR were leveraged in the development of this study’s contributions: (1) a conceptual framework—IA-AIIF—to guide the adoption of emerging AI technologies within internal audits; (2) the Internal Audit—AI Integration Assessment Cube (IA-AI Cube); and (3) a Hierarchical Weighted Scoring Model (HWSM)—a mathematical construct, enabling the evaluation of AI integration levels and their footprint within a company’s IAF. The analytical assessment of the proposed IA-AIIF is based on the conceptual model validation approach proposed by Sargent [[33](https://www.mdpi.com#B33-electronics-15-02280)], using the trace validation technique. This technique traces the behavior of specific components throughout the framework to verify the correctness of its logic and the accuracy of the simulated outcomes. According to [[33](https://www.mdpi.com#B33-electronics-15-02280)], the conceptual model validation is addressed by evaluating whether the assumptions, structure, and relationships of the framework are reasonable for the intended purpose and consistent with accepted domain knowledge. A comparative analysis against the Gartner maturity model seeks to position the IA-AI Cube within the existing landscape of maturity frameworks, thereby illustrating how it addresses an identified gap by integrating maturity, internal audit functions, and technology into a single formalized construct. The HWSM is assessed for conceptual validity through two complementary simulation-based analyses: (1) a scenario-based sensitivity analysis, to examine the model’s responsiveness to controlled changes in dimensional weights and explore its ability to capture shifting strategic priorities for an organization; (2) a parallel benchmark comparison with a Conventional Equal-Weight Model (CEWM) to assess whether HWSM captures domain-specific priorities more effectively than CEWM. The main purpose of this approach is to obtain preliminary evidence of the model’s mathematical coherence, discriminatory capability, and practical adaptability for assessing AI maturity within the IAF. The validation strategy is intended to establish methodological plausibility and design coherence, with findings indicating theoretical applicability under simulated conditions.

## 4. Results

### 4.1. The Internal Audit–Artificial Intelligence Integration Framework (IA-AIIF)

Drawing on the findings of the literature review, this study proposes a framework for integrating emerging AI technologies into organizational IT systems to enhance the effectiveness, efficiency, and value of internal audit activities. This framework is structured around five AI-enabled capabilities areas for the IAF:

Automating repetitive internal auditing tasks: Minimizing manual effort for data extraction, working paper documentation, and standard report generation.

Advanced anomaly and risk detection: Leveraging LLM reasoning to identify latent inconsistencies, potential fraud patterns, and non-compliance risks in organizations’ documents and records that traditional sampling might overlook.

Augmented decision-making: Streamlining strategic choices by synthesizing real-time data, applying predictive analytics, and delivering actionable insights from complex datasets.

Continuous regulatory compliance: Ensuring dynamic alignment with evolving accounting and auditing standards (e.g., the Institute of Internal Auditors, International Standards on Auditing) through continuous monitoring of external regulatory repositories.

Organizational memory and learning via RAG: Using RAG and VecDBs to consolidate historical audit findings and internal policies into a secure, searchable organizational knowledge base, thereby supporting professional development and institutional knowledge retention.

The integrated framework comprises six primary, interconnected layers, as presented in [Figure 2](https://www.mdpi.com#electronics-15-02280-f002): (1) Filtering (Security) Layer, (2) Knowledge Layer (Advanced RAG), (3) Agentic AI Layer, (4) Integration Layer (MCP), (5) Data Platform Layer (DataLake), and (6) Data Sources and Applications Layer. It is designed to automate complex internal audit workflows while maintaining rigorous security standards.

Filtering (Security) Layer: This critical component serves as a “security gatekeeper” between the internal auditor and the LLM. It anonymizes and redacts sensitive data (contracts, trade secrets, etc.) and performs Data Loss Prevention (DLP) checks. It ensures that any prompt sent to the LLM, especially when interacting with external specialist systems, is stripped of confidential identifiers while preserving the context needed for analysis.

Knowledge Layer (Advanced RAG): This layer transforms heterogeneous and unstructured audit data into a structured form of “organizational memory” that can be accessed by AI models. It uses document processing techniques such as chunking and embedding creation to break complex audit documents into manageable segments and convert them into vector representations. These vectors are stored in a VecDB, enabling quick, meaningful semantic searches [[65](https://www.mdpi.com#B65-electronics-15-02280)]. To ensure high-quality outputs, a ranking component (reranker) prioritizes the most relevant evidence before it is provided to the LLM. It helps reduce noise, improve precision, and minimize the risk of hallucinations [[59](https://www.mdpi.com#B59-electronics-15-02280)]. In IAF, this layer plays a critical role by anchoring AI-generated outputs to authoritative organizational sources, such as policies, procedures, contracts, and prior audit reports. Unlike a standalone LLM, which depends mainly on pre-trained knowledge and can produce plausible but unverified responses, a RAG-enhanced system retrieves and uses domain-specific evidence during the query process [[57](https://www.mdpi.com#B57-electronics-15-02280)]. This significantly improves transparency, traceability, and compliance with internal audit standards or internal rules while also reducing the risk of hallucinations. As a result, RAG facilitates the shift from generic AI support to context-aware, evidence-based audit analysis.

Integration Layer (MCP): This layer serves as an intermediary enabling communication between the Knowledge Layer and external or internal data sources. MCP servers provide controlled access to the organization’s tools, databases, and services [[68](https://www.mdpi.com#B68-electronics-15-02280)], enabling AI agents to fetch data and execute operations in real time. Within the IAF, this layer is essential to preserve data integrity and traceability as part of a mature data governance framework. By implementing controlled access mechanisms and maintaining a clear interaction log, this integration layer will enable the audit of AI-driven actions and will improve the transparency, reproducibility, and segregation of duties, as a key audit principle.

Agentic AI Layer: Implementing this layer will serve as an orchestration core for the IA-AIIF. It can guide the reasoning and coordination that help automate the audit process. Unlike a standard LLM, which mainly produces responses from prompts, the Agentic AI layer is able to plan toward goals, reason iteratively, and make decisions dynamically [[70](https://www.mdpi.com#B70-electronics-15-02280)]. It decomposes complex audit objectives into structured tasks and identifies the relevant data sources and tools to be used, including those enabled through RAG or MCP. These tasks and actions are then sequenced into a coherent workflow designed to support the execution of a specific audit goal. By continuously assessing intermediate outcomes and adjusting its actions, the AI agent can approximate human audit reasoning. It is designed to support the transition from static, rule-based audit procedures toward automated and, at higher maturity levels, semi-autonomous audit processes.

Data Platform Layer (DataLake): This layer is the central repository for the organization’s structured and unstructured data. It serves as the single source of truth (SSOT) from which the knowledge layer draws information for processing.

Data Sources and Applications Layer: This is the foundational data ingestion tier. It features the Knowledge Consolidator, which integrates external regulatory data (IIA Standards, ISA regulations, industry benchmarks, legal updates, etc.) and internal audit data (historical internal audit reports, applications transaction logs, risk and control matrices, etc.). The integration of Internal and External APIs allows the system to interface directly with live business applications.

**Figure 2.** A multi-layered structure of the proposed Internal Audit–Artificial Intelligence Integration Framework (IA-AIIF). **Figure 2.** A multi-layered structure of the proposed Internal Audit–Artificial Intelligence Integration Framework (IA-AIIF).   The operational workflow of the IA-AIIF ([Figure 3](https://www.mdpi.com#electronics-15-02280-f003)) is described in the following sequence:

Internal Audit Request and Evidence Submission: The internal auditor submits a complex prompt (e.g.,  “Analyze procurement logs for potential duplicate payments” ) or requests an action (e.g.,  “Generate an internal audit finding” ) and provides relevant evidence, such as log files, PDF contracts, etc.

Security Sanitization: The Filtering (Security) Layer intercepts the request, stripping sensitive identifiers. At the same time, it preserves the semantic context required for the AI to understand the internal audit objective.

Agentic Task Planning: The Agentic AI Layer decomposes the internal auditor’s request into sub-tasks. It determines if additional historical context, retrieved from the DataLake, or live data from production systems via the Integration Layer is needed.

Advanced RAG: For historical context, the system triggers the Knowledge Layer. Data are chunked, embedded, and queried via Semantic Search. The Results Ranker filters the best evidence to be sent to the LLM (Generator).

Real-time Tool Execution (MCP): If live data are required, the Agent utilizes MCP to query Internal/External APIs. This allows the system to compare almost instantly “what should be” (standards) with “what is” (live system transactions).

Synthesis and Response Generation: The LLM (Generator) synthesizes the ranked RAG fragments and the live MCP data. It identifies anomalies and drafts an internal audit report, citing the retrieved evidence.

Feedback and Continuous Learning Loop: The internal auditor reviews the output and provides feedback (e.g., confirming suspected fraud or correcting a misinterpreted control). This feedback is routed back through the Knowledge Consolidator to refine the VecDB. This ensures the system “learns” organizational nuances and improves its precision for the next internal audit mission without leaking data to external training sets.

 4.1.1. Critical Analysis of the Operational Feasibility and Scalability of the Proposed IA-AIIF

The comparative analysis of existing frameworks ([Table 2](https://www.mdpi.com#electronics-15-02280-t002)) reveals two structural gaps that the IA-AIIF is intended to address. First, we did not find, within the reviewed frameworks (including the IIA’s AI Auditing Framework, NIST AI RMF, and COSO’s GenAI guidance), a technological architecture that connects AI reasoning capabilities to live organizational data streams. Second, existing frameworks operate at a principle-based level, providing guidance without specifying the mechanisms through which AI outputs are grounded in evidence, thereby leaving hallucination risk unaddressed. The IA-AIIF addresses both gaps simultaneously through its layered architecture, distinguishing it structurally from prior contributions. The IA-AIIF’s operational feasibility depends on several organizational prerequisites, including digital access to audit-relevant data sources and the availability of repositories for structured and unstructured evidence. From an operational feasibility perspective, each layer of the IA-AIIF corresponds to technologies with demonstrated industrial applicability. RAG architectures have been shown to reduce hallucination rates from approximately 33% to single-digit percentages in cross-domain applications [[59](https://www.mdpi.com#B59-electronics-15-02280)]. MCP has been adopted by leading AI platforms as a standardized integration protocol, with over 8000 active servers documented in recent analyses [[74](https://www.mdpi.com#B74-electronics-15-02280)]. Agentic AI frameworks have demonstrated effectiveness in decomposing complex, multi-step tasks in unpredictable environments [[70](https://www.mdpi.com#B70-electronics-15-02280)]. These findings support the operational plausibility of the IA-AIIF architecture, even in the absence of a dedicated internal audit deployment. Regarding the scalability of the IA-AIIF, the use of VecDBs with ANN indexing enables semantic retrieval across large, heterogeneous data repositories without the computational cost of a brute-force search. This will increase scalability as organizational data lakes continue to grow. Additionally, the IA-AIIF could also be considered vertically scalable because it enables a staged evolution from descriptive analytics and assisted audit procedures toward predictive, prescriptive, and semi-autonomous audit capabilities. These elements provide the necessary premises for assessing AI integration maturity in IAF, as presented in the following sections.

### 4.2. A Multi-Dimensional Assessment Model for Intelligent Internal Audits

 4.2.1. Acknowledging the Research Context and Gaps

AI technologies in internal auditing currently facilitate the automation of audit procedures and the detection of anomalies in financial data. However, these operations are often fragmented, focusing separately on technological capabilities, audit functions, or maturity models. Our paper addresses the need for an integrated, multidimensional model to assess AI maturity in the IAF. It examines the relationships among its maturity levels, various audit areas, and supporting technologies.

 4.2.2. A Proposed Model for Assessing the AI Maturity Level in the Internal Audit Function: The Internal Audit–AI Integration Assessment Cube (IA-AI Cube)

This study introduces the IA-AI Cube ([Figure 4](https://www.mdpi.com#electronics-15-02280-f004)), defined as a three-dimensional conceptual model that structures AI-enabled internal audit capabilities at the intersection of the following:

Internal audit maturity—internal audit capabilities based on AI emerging technologies;

Internal audit functions—organizational objectives;

AI-enabled technological layers—the implementation mechanisms.

Formally, the model can be expressed as    IA − AIC = f(M, F, T)  where

M ∈ {L1, L2, L3, L4, L5} represents internal audit maturity levels;

F ∈ {F1, F2, F3} represents internal audit functions;

T ∈ {T1, T2, T3, T4, T5, T6} represents technological layers.

Each combination (M, F, T) defines a distinct AI audit capability unit.  **Figure 4.** Internal Audit—AI Integration Assessment Cube (IA-AI Cube). **Figure 4.** Internal Audit—AI Integration Assessment Cube (IA-AI Cube).  

 Dimension 1: Internal Audit Maturity

The maturity dimension reflects the progressive evolution toward an intelligent internal audit. While prior research defines analytics maturity [[99](https://www.mdpi.com#B99-electronics-15-02280)] and explores AI applications in internal audits [[69](https://www.mdpi.com#B69-electronics-15-02280)], the proposed framework extends these models by introducing an additional Autonomous Audit stage and embedding the maturity progression within the internal audit functional and technological dimensions.

L1—Descriptive level: focusing on reporting, dashboards, and retrospective analysis. AI primarily handles data visualization and basic summarization. At this level of AI use in IAF, audit evidence is mostly retrospective, requiring auditors to manually interpret insights.

L2—Diagnostic level: identification of root causes through analytical methods. The diagnostic level provides analytical depth, allowing auditors to examine the root causes of identified issues. Using AI for data analytics will help identify patterns, deviations, and causal relationships, but understanding still largely depends on human expertise.

L3—Predictive level: anticipation of risks using statistical and machine learning models. AI can identify emerging risks, potential control failures, or fraud indicators before they happen [[100](https://www.mdpi.com#B100-electronics-15-02280)]. At this level, the IAF takes on a proactive role by incorporating risk assessment capabilities using LLM, predictive analytics, and probabilistic risk scoring.

L4—Prescriptive level: AI-generated recommendations and decision support level. The prescriptive level incorporates decision intelligence, where AI systems not only forecast outcomes but also suggest optimal actions. By adopting AI-generated tools, auditors transition from simply executing procedural tasks to becoming strategic advisors. At this level of maturity, the internal audit shifts to an advisory and strategic role, enabling auditors to offer management actionable recommendations for next steps to achieve desired outcomes [[101](https://www.mdpi.com#B101-electronics-15-02280)].

L5—Autonomous Audit level. The highest maturity level represents the evolution toward semi-autonomous or fully autonomous audit systems. At this level, the AI continuously monitors processes, detects anomalies, initiates audit procedures and, in some cases, triggers corrective actions. Continuous monitoring is valuable for detecting and resolving control failures more quickly, performing dynamic risk assessments, reducing audit lag, and shortening cycle times [[102](https://www.mdpi.com#B102-electronics-15-02280)]. The primary implication for human auditors is a transition from execution-oriented tasks to roles centered on oversight, validation, and governance of AI-driven systems.

The Internal Audit Maturity Dimension builds upon the established analytics maturity model while extending its application to the internal audit domain, representing an original contribution of this study to the field.

 Dimension 2: Internal Audit Functions

The functional dimension of the IA-AI Cube is grounded in the framework of the Institute of Internal Auditors (IIA), particularly the International Professional Practices Framework (IPPF) and the Global Internal Audit Standards [[87](https://www.mdpi.com#B87-electronics-15-02280)]. The IIA states that an internal audit is responsible for assessing and enhancing the effectiveness of governance, risk management, and control systems. These three domains form the core functional scope of internal audit activities. Consequently, this standard breaks down the functional dimension into three items:

F1—Conformity with internal procedures and regulations, ensuring compliance with internal policies and regulatory frameworks.

F2—Risk Management: identification, assessment, and mitigation of organizational risks.

F3—Corporate Governance: evaluation of governance structures, decision-making processes, and accountability mechanisms.

This classification aligns with international audit standards (e.g., the IIA framework) and ensures functional completeness.

 Dimension 3: AI-Enabled Technology Layers

The third dimension of the IA-AI Cube captures the technological stack that enables AI-driven internal audit capabilities. This dimension is strongly related to the proposed IA-AIIF framework (see [Figure 3](https://www.mdpi.com#electronics-15-02280-f003)), aiming to incorporate AI into internal audits.

T1—Data Acquisition and Integration: data ingestion, ETL processes, and integration across systems. This layer serves as the foundation of the IAF, focusing on collecting, importing, and merging data from various sources.

T2—AI Analytics: machine learning, statistical models, anomaly detection. This layer introduces analytical processing capabilities, transforming raw data into identifiable patterns, correlations, and anomalies using statistical and AI techniques.

T3—AI Audit Intelligence: risk scoring, predictive insights, and knowledge inference. This layer implements the shift from analytics to intelligence, where awareness is put into context and turned into internal audit-relevant knowledge.

T4—AI Audit Automation and Augmentation: automation of internal audit tasks and AI-assisted auditor workflows. It focuses on the execution of audit activities, combining automation with AI-driven augmentation of human auditors’ decisions.

T5—AI Reporting and Decision Support: dashboards, automated reporting, and decision intelligence. This component translates internal audit insights into actionable information for decision-makers, integrating analytics with business context.

T6—AI Continuous Auditing: represents the evolution toward real-time, continuous assurance, where AI-guided internal audit processes are integrated into operational systems.

This third dimension reflects a layered architecture consistent with a modern data and AI environment.

 Analytical Interpretation of the IA-AI Cube

Scholars have proposed a modular framework for integrating Agentic AI across diverse research areas [[57](https://www.mdpi.com#B57-electronics-15-02280)]. Ref. [[35](https://www.mdpi.com#B35-electronics-15-02280)] examined how different categories of AI, specifically assisted, augmented, and autonomous systems, influence the internal audit performance of companies. However, to our knowledge, no studies have proposed a multidimensional model for assessing the integration of emerging AI technologies into the IAF. The IA-AI Cube addresses this gap and enables multiple analytical perspectives: (a) Horizontal analysis (Maturity evolution), tracking the progression from a descriptive to autonomous audit within a given function and technology. (b) Vertical analysis (Functional coverage), evaluating how AI capabilities are distributed across the internal audit functions dimension. (c) Depth analysis (Technological sophistication), assessing the adoption of advanced technologies across maturity levels and the internal audit functions dimension.

 4.2.3. Comparative Analysis: Gartner’s AI Maturity Model vs. IA-AI Cube’s Maturity Model

To contextualize the evolution of AI within the IAF, it is essential to differentiate between organizational technology adoption and domain-specific functional maturity. Gartner’s AI Maturity Model [[103](https://www.mdpi.com#B103-electronics-15-02280)] offers a macro-level perspective on enterprise-wide AI integration, emphasizing technology deployment, governance structures, and business value creation. In contrast, the IA-AI Cube Maturity Model focuses on the analytical depth of internal audit activities and the evolving cognitive role of the internal auditor. As shown in [Table 6](https://www.mdpi.com#electronics-15-02280-t006), there is a correlation between Gartner’s stages and the functional levels of the proposed Maturity model. The main value-generating activities (Primary Value Driver) and the internal auditor’s role at each level have been identified and included in the table. The comparison highlights a fundamental difference in the “human-in-the-loop” changes. While Gartner’s model focuses on organizational coordination, change management, and return on investment, the IA-AI Cube model tracks the progressive delegation of cognitive tasks from the internal auditor to AI systems. A critical transition occurs at the Operational/Predictive intersection, where the internal auditor shifts from retrospective analysis to forward-looking risk anticipation. The most pronounced divergence appears between the highest maturity levels of the two models. Gartner defines the Transformational stage primarily in terms of competitive advantage and enterprise reinvention, whereas the IA-AI Cube model conceptualizes this stage as the Autonomous Internal Audit. In the latter, the function evolves toward a “self-healing” control environment in which AI systems initiate audit procedures, flag anomalies in real time, and recommend remedial actions. In such an environment, the internal auditor assumes a higher-order role focused on algorithmic accountability, ethical oversight, strategic judgment, and AI governance. Consequently, achieving Level 4 or 5 in the IA-AI Cube model is typically contingent upon the organization reaching at least the Operational stage in Gartner’s maturity model. From a managerial perspective, the successful transition from diagnostic to prescriptive audit capabilities depends heavily on executive sponsorship and clearly established ownership of AI initiatives; without these, substantial implementation barriers are likely to emerge. Gartner’s AI Maturity Model provides a macro-level perspective on enterprise-wide AI adoption but does not capture the domain-specific cognitive evolution of the internal auditor nor the functional scope of the IAF. The IA-AI Cube addresses a structural gap in existing maturity and assessment frameworks by simultaneously integrating three dimensions (maturity, internal audit functions, and technology) into a single formalized construct.

This score indicates the extent to which the components of each model dimension are represented within the specific assessment context. The score for each item in the IA-AI Cube should be grounded in evidence, such as audit methodology, system documentation, dashboards and reports, auditor interviews, internal procedures, AI models used for internal audit, etc. Therefore, while the IA-AI Cube provides a conceptual, multidimensional model of audit capabilities (maturity × function × technology), its practical implementation requires a layered system framework, as illustrated in [Figure 3](https://www.mdpi.com#electronics-15-02280-f003). ## 5. Discussions and Study Implications

Although AI has attracted growing attention from both researchers and practitioners due to its significant impact across every aspect of an organization, only a limited number of studies have explored its integration into the IAF. Responding to the research gap identified by Wassie and Lakatos [[18](https://www.mdpi.com#B18-electronics-15-02280)] regarding the shortage of comprehensive AI frameworks for internal audit, our study proposes an integrated architectural model to operationalize AI-driven audit processes. Prior research primarily discusses AI adoption from a managerial or conceptual general perspective, without including the technological dimension in the proposed framework [[18](https://www.mdpi.com#B18-electronics-15-02280)]. The authors have discussed the use of AI in internal audits, proposing a framework emphasizing commitment, access, capability, and skilling (CACS) as the four essential pillars for the successful use of technology in audit workflows. Another AI auditing framework with real-time monitoring and predictive auditing capabilities, without a focus on the field of internal auditing, was introduced by [[46](https://www.mdpi.com#B46-electronics-15-02280)]. However, these frameworks overlook the interoperability and integration of emerging AI technologies within the IAF—a gap we aim to fill. From a technological point of view, although we identified studies proposing agentic AI architectures integrated with RAG and MCP for deterministic engineering environments [[57](https://www.mdpi.com#B57-electronics-15-02280)], the authors focus on physics-based computation, numerical correctness, and infrastructure lifecycle management and not on internal auditing. To the best of our knowledge, the existing internal audit literature has not yet proposed a unified multi-layered architectural framework integrating semantic knowledge retrieval, standardized AI-system interoperability, and agentic reasoning capabilities into a coherent model for autonomous internal auditing. Our proposed framework operationalizes AI integration in internal auditing through a unified architecture that combines RAG, vector databases, the MCP, and Agentic AI capabilities. Compared with previous studies, our framework recontextualizes these technologies for the internal audit domain by introducing a semantic organizational memory, enterprise-wide interoperability, and AI-driven audit orchestration. Furthermore, we propose a dedicated knowledge layer that structures heterogeneous organizational data into auditable, semantically searchable representations using embeddings and vector-based retrieval. While using AI can be seen as an initial step, integrating emerging AI technologies into the organization’s IT infrastructure is a more advanced and comprehensive approach. This strategy helps the IAF stay aligned with new technological trends. We also adhere to the view, as outlined in prior research, that AI should not replace internal auditors. Rather, it is a valuable tool for handling routine tasks, freeing humans to focus on high-value, strategic advisory functions. There is a broad consensus in the literature and practice on AI’s transformative impact on the IAF. However, there remains a significant lack of frameworks and models specifically tailored to the unique professional context of AI-assisted internal auditing, particularly for assessing maturity levels. A structured framework for integrating AI into the IAF’s organizational information system has proven essential for transitioning from traditional, manual processes to a proactive, value-added assurance model. To answer research question RQ1, we introduced the IA-AIIF framework, designed to integrate emerging AI technologies into an organization’s internal audit landscape to be used through a hybrid approach, the AI-copiloted internal audit. The framework is further expanded by a multidimensional measurement model formalized as a mathematical construct, enabling the evaluation of AI integration levels and their footprint within a company’s internal audit function. Therefore, the IA-AI Cube and HWSM model were developed to answer research question RQ2. The IA-AI Cube represents an original contribution to the field. It is a conceptual and operational model that connects traditional internal auditing methods with AI-driven innovation by structuring audit capabilities across maturity, function, and technology dimensions. By extending this model to an n-dimensional hypercube, the generalized model enables modular integration of additional vectors, such as regulatory compliance, ethical risk, and real-time data streaming. Additionally, the proposed scoring model (HWSM) operationalizes the IA-AI Cube, converting it from a conceptual model into a practical assessment tool. The aggregate score (AS), calculated using HWSM, measures and compares the levels of AI integration maturity within the IAF. It enables organizations to identify technological and functional gaps and prioritize AI investments in internal auditing. The proposed IA-AI Cube introduces several innovative contributions: (1) a multi-dimensional integration model that simultaneously combines maturity, function, and technology; (2) a formalization of AI audit capability units, where each cube cell represents a measurable and actionable unit, facilitating benchmarking, capability mapping, and structured implementation; (3) a method to evaluate the transition from static to dynamic auditing, conceptualizing the evolution toward continuous auditing, real-time risk monitoring, and AI-driven decisions; and (4) a foundation for maturity assessment tools, serving as the basis for internal audit AI maturity models, scoring systems, and digital transformation roadmaps.

### 5.1. The IA-AIIF Framework—Usage Scenario and Trace Validation

To improve clarity on how the IA-AIIF framework can be implemented by organizations, we provide a detailed usage scenario below. This scenario serves a dual purpose: illustrating the framework’s practical applicability and assessing its operational logic through trace validation, consistent with the simulation-based validation approach described in [Section 4.1.2](https://www.mdpi.com#sec4dot1dot2-electronics-15-02280). An organization’s internal audit department wants to assess the procurement cycle. The general purpose is to identify control weaknesses, detect unusual transactions, and verify whether purchases above specific thresholds were approved in accordance with internal policy. Traditionally, this engagement would require auditors to manually collect data from several systems. This may come from various documents from business units, from comparing transactions against procedures, or from test samples.

 5.1.1. Characteristics of the Considered Synthetic Dataset

Using the IA-AIIF framework, data are collected from various operational systems and external data, including both structured data and unstructured documents relevant to the audit. We consider a multidimensional dataset that replicates the complexity of a corporate procurement cycle, structured as follows: The Unstructured Knowledge Base: We consider a repository of 50 synthetic documents, including the following:

Procurement policies (150+ pages, defining a €5k limit for junior buyers, defining a €10k threshold, and specifying a mandatory three-quote rule);

Internal delegation of authority matrices;

Purchase requests and approval workflows (documents containing timestamps and digital signatures of designated approvers);

Vendor quotes (multiple bids received for each significant acquisition, including the selection report for the winning offer);

Vendor contracts.

We assume these documents contain overlapping or specific clauses (e.g., varying approval thresholds) to test the Advanced RAG’s ability to retrieve the most legally binding “chunk” of information. The Transactional Data Pool: We assume a dataset of 5000 procurement records. These data are stored in internal databases and include “hidden” anomalies that serve as the ground truth for our testing logic:

Split-billing (multiple invoices just below the €10k threshold to circumvent senior management approval);

Duplicate payments (identical amounts paid to the same vendor under different invoice references);

Process circumvention (transactions where the final payment does not correlate with the designated winning offer or where the purchase request was approved by an unauthorized person).

This information is consolidated into a unified analytical data repository within the data lake layer, where structured data are stored in datasets, while unstructured files are maintained together with metadata, provenance information, and access control mechanisms. The data lake acts as a single source of truth for the audit system to retrieve past records, reconcile data, and establish a consistent evidence base.

 5.1.2. Simulation of Framework Execution and Expected Results

By applying the IA-AIIF logic to the aforementioned data, we can trace the expected flow of an automated internal audit mission ([Table 8](https://www.mdpi.com#electronics-15-02280-t008)). It should be noted that [Table 8](https://www.mdpi.com#electronics-15-02280-t008) presents theoretically expected outputs derived from the framework’s logic applied to synthetic data.[Table 8](https://www.mdpi.com#electronics-15-02280-t008) presents expected outputs derived from the IA-AIIF logic applied to synthetic data. These outcomes have not been empirically validated in a live internal audit environment. To illustrate the transition from conceptual AI integration layers to real audit workflows, the following section traces the IA-AIIF operations across the four standard phases of an internal audit engagement.

 Phase 1: Internal Audit Planning

In the planning phase, the internal auditor defines the internal audit objective and materiality threshold. In this scenario, the objective is to identify procurement transactions above €10k that may have circumvented approval policies. Rather than manually reviewing 5k transaction records and 50 policy documents, the auditor queries the IA-AIIF system: “ Retrieve all applicable approval thresholds and identify transactions that may conflict with them ”. The Data Sources Layer indexes the 5k records from ERP and 50 policy documents into the data lake. The Knowledge Layer semantically retrieves the relevant policy clauses, specifically the three-quote rule and the €5k/€10k authority matrices, and makes them available to the LLM as grounded context. At this stage, the internal auditor’s role shifts from manually collecting data to establishing objectives and formulating queries, thereby reducing planning time and expanding the range of evidence evaluated.

 Phase 2: Investigation and Evidence Gathering

The Agentic AI Layer decomposes the auditor’s objective into three parallel sub-tasks: (1) cross-referencing each transaction above €10,000 against the three-quote requirement; (2) flagging transactions approved by individuals whose authority level is below the applicable threshold; and (3) identifying invoice amounts that diverge from the winning vendor offer on record. The MCP Integration Layer connects the AI agents to SQL databases in real time, retrieving approval logs. In the simulated scenario, this process identifies that Project Alpha has only one vendor quote on file instead of three and that the final invoice of €12k exceeds the winning offer of €8k, a €4k unexplained overrun. At each intermediate step, the system presents the retrieved evidence, specific document chunks, transaction records, and approval logs. This maintains a sense of professional skepticism within an automated process.

 Phase 3: Analysis and Risk Assessment

In the analysis phase, the Knowledge Layer generates a Risk Score of 0.98 for Project Alpha, flagging “ Unauthorized Approval and Budget Overrun ” due to a combination of missing quotes and invoice discrepancies. The auditor validates the AI’s reasoning in cases of justified exceptions (e.g., emergency procurement). The auditor’s role here is interpretive rather than computational: they assess whether the flagged anomaly reflects a genuine control failure, a documentation gap, or a legitimate exception requiring contextual judgment. For example, the auditor may decide that a single submitted quotation is acceptable when the procurement is urgent, since this assessment requires contextual business judgment that exceeds the AI system’s reasoning capacity. This “human-in-the-loop” validation step is structurally embedded in the IA-AIIF workflow, consistent with the “copiloted auditing” model described in [Section 2.3](https://www.mdpi.com#sec2dot3-electronics-15-02280).

 Phase 4: Reporting

In the reporting phase, the LLM integrates ranked evidence fragments with current transaction data to produce a structured preliminary internal audit finding. This references both the specific policy clauses obtained by the RAG system and the transaction records accessed via MCP from the Data Sources Layer. The auditor reviews, contextualizes, and concludes the findings before inclusion in the final audit report. The Feedback and Continuous Learning Loop routes the auditor’s corrections back through the Knowledge Consolidator, refining the VecDB for subsequent audit missions. Over time, this mechanism is designed to consolidate individual audit engagements into an organizational memory. It may progressively improve the LLM’s reasoning accuracy by enabling the system to learn from organization-specific audit knowledge. At the same time, it ensures that sensitive data remains protected from exposure outside the organization. The presented use case relies on synthetic data and simulated framework execution. While designed to mirror realistic procurement scenarios, the results should be interpreted as illustrative of the IA-AIIF intended logic rather than as empirical evidence of its effectiveness.

### 5.2. An Explanatory Usage Scenario for the IA-AI Cube and HWSM Models

The Hierarchical Weighted Scoring Model (HWSM) is implemented through a structured, four-phase operational procedure. By adopting a contingency-based weighting approach, the model ensures that audit assessments remain dynamic and aligned with the organization’s strategic priorities.

 5.2.1. The Organizational Calibration Procedure

Recognizing that the relative importance of internal audit dimensions and sub-dimensions is inherently context-dependent, the HWSM incorporates an internal calibration phase rather than relying on static weights. This calibration is conducted via a consensus-based method, such as the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) or expert point allocation, within dedicated assessment workshops involving key decision-makers (e.g., Chief Audit Executives, Risk Management Executives, IT governance leads, etc.). This participatory approach ensures that the model reflects the organization’s current strategic vision, thereby enhancing the relevance of the final aggregate score (AS).

 5.2.2. Practical Implementation Workflow

To enhance clarity and showcase its reusability, the following practical example illustrates a systematic workflow for using the model:

Key decision-maker alignment (Phase I): Define the internal audit scope and convene a cross-functional panel to establish the relative importance of the three cube dimensions. In real-world internal audit environments, not all AI capabilities contribute equally to organizational performance and audit effectiveness. Certain advanced capabilities, such as predictive analytics, continuous auditing, and AI-driven decision support, tend to generate significantly greater strategic and operational impact. By contrast, foundational capabilities such as reporting or data acquisition, while essential as enabling components, typically provide more limited transformational value when considered in isolation. In this phase, key decision-makers assess the three dimensions of the IA-AI Cube: IA maturity, audit functions, and technology layers. They answer questions such as:  “What is the relative importance of the technological dimension compared to the maturity of internal auditing?”  or  “How much more important is Predictive AI compared to Descriptive AI in evaluating IA maturity?” .

Weight Allocation (Phase II): The pool of key decision-makers uses the selected weighting method to assign numerical values to Wi ([Table 9](https://www.mdpi.com#electronics-15-02280-t009)) and wi,j, ensuring that the sum constraints are maintained (Sum(Wi) = 1 and Sum(wi,j) = 1).

 Comparative Analysis of HWSM and the Conventional Equal-Weight Model (CEWM)

To validate the HWSM’s differentiation capability, the results calculated using the HWSM equations are compared with those obtained using the Conventional Equal-Weight Model (CEWM). In CEWM, all dimensions and sub-dimensions are treated as having equal importance (Wi = 0.33 and wi,j = constant). The previously established scores for each sub-dimension remain unchanged. Applying the algorithm, AS calculated using CEWM is 1.85 (see [Table 13](https://www.mdpi.com#electronics-15-02280-t013)). The comparative analysis ([Table 14](https://www.mdpi.com#electronics-15-02280-t014)) reveals that the conventional, non-weighted approach yields a deceptively higher score (1.85). This is due to the “masking effect”, where high scores in basic data integration (T1) and conformity (F1) artificially inflate the overall result, hiding the severe lack of advanced maturity (D1). The equal-weight model implicitly assumes that all dimensions and components contribute equally to AI maturity in internal audits. By contrast, the HWSM has the potential to improve granularity, as seen in the Audit Maturity score, compared with CEWM. The Audit Maturity Score (D1) obtained through HWSM is lower than the one obtained through CEWM because the higher the weight (w1,j) allocated to the sub-dimensions of the maturity level (L1-L5), the more likely the failure to reach the maximum score of 3 (described in Phase III above) results in a greater penalty.

### 5.3. Limitations of the Study

Regarding the literature review, we acknowledge limitations largely shaped by the study’s exclusion criteria applied throughout the selection process. The search was confined to the Web of Science (WoS) databases, thereby precluding consideration of other potentially relevant databases. Additionally, the corpus was limited to English-language sources, excluding non-English publications. It should also be noted that the formulation of search terms and the construction of the search string are inherently subjective. Consequently, some relevant studies might not be retrieved. Notwithstanding these constraints, the analyzed corpus is considered sufficiently representative of the research scope and provides a robust foundation for the present investigation. Although the IA-AIIF offers, from our perspective, valuable conceptual and architectural insights, several limitations need to be recognized. The study introduces a conceptual architecture rather than a fully implemented industrial solution. While the framework proposes the integration of emerging AI technologies into an internal audit-oriented system, its operational effectiveness in real-world organizations requires empirical validation through pilot implementations. The intrinsic probabilistic nature of LLMs, part of the IA-AIIF proposed framework, constitutes another pertinent concern. The Knowledge Layer mitigates the risk of LLM hallucinations by grounding the system’s reasoning in the organization’s actual governance framework. This layer does not fully eliminate the risk, because RAG reduces uncertainty by anchoring responses to retrieved documents. However, RAG does not completely change the LLM’s probabilistic nature. Because conclusions in internal auditing must remain explainable, reproducible, and defensible from a compliance perspective [[104](https://www.mdpi.com#B104-electronics-15-02280)], this limitation is particularly important. The above scenarios illustrate the use of the IA-AIIF and the HWSM. While this approach provides evidence of their logical coherence and internal consistency, one must keep in mind the limitations of a simulated scenario. Transforming this research result into an appropriate Proof of Concept would require substantial resources, grouped into three areas: (1) financial, including operational costs for the IT infrastructure, data lake maintenance, etc.; (2) human capital, requiring deep internal audit expertise combined with advanced technical skills in prompt engineering, agent orchestration, and data governance; and (3) time, as the full project lifecycle, from MCP integration to agent calibration, typically extends well beyond the scope of a standard academic research timeline. Another limitation of the proposed framework is the potential for technological obsolescence. As AI technologies and architectures evolve rapidly, some components and connections within the framework may become outdated, necessitating continuous updates and adjustments. Similarly, the AI Audit Cube has not yet been validated through large-scale empirical implementation across multiple organizations and industries. Another limitation concerns the consistency of the assessment process over time. As exemplified in the simulated scenario, the model relies on evaluator-based scoring. Therefore, a solution to ensure coherence across assessment periods could be to develop a detailed, consistently applied standard operating procedure (SOP). This SOP should clearly specify the criteria, evidence, and methods used for scoring each sub-dimension. Despite these limitations, the study serves as a solid theoretical blueprint for upcoming empirical pilot initiatives.

## 6. Conclusions

### 6.1. Theoretical and Methodological Contributions

This study proposes a multi-layered, AI-driven framework, the IA-AIIF, designed to enhance the IAF by supporting the advance from traditional, manual audit methodologies toward more automated and, at higher maturity levels, semi-autonomous audit processes. Unlike previous studies that address AI as a standalone tool, the IA-AIIF is designed to enable an integrated approach, in which AI technologies function as a “copilot” supporting the internal auditors in their activities. The IA-AIIF has the potential to orchestrate multi-step internal audit activities by linking internal audit reasoning to authoritative organizational evidence, such as internal policies, procedures, prior audit reports, and other documentation. By incorporating a knowledge layer, supported by technologies such as RAG, VecDbs, semantic search, and LLMs, the framework creates the conditions for more evidence-grounded internal audit analysis. This capability of the IA-AIIF is particularly relevant in mitigating known limitations of LLM-based systems, such as hallucinations, by anchoring AI-generated outputs in trusted organizational data sources. This study also contributes to the field by proposing the IA-AI Cube as a multidimensional assessment model for evaluating the degree of AI integration within the IAF. A distinct methodological contribution is the HWSM, which moves beyond one-dimensional maturity assessments by introducing hierarchical weighting. The IA-AI Cube, in conjunction with the HWSM scoring model, functions as a strategic diagnostic tool. The internal consistency of the HWSM was assessed through a dual-validation approach conducted during the usage scenario exercises.

### 6.2. Practical Implications

The IA-AIIF may offer organizations a pathway to modernize their internal audit operating model by integrating AI-enabled capabilities into audit planning, evidence collection, analytical procedures, and reporting. By employing IA-AIIF, an organization can improve the consistency, traceability, and evidence-based quality of internal audit outputs. The IA-AI Cube and HWSM may help organizations distinguish between adopting isolated AI tools and developing genuinely integrated internal audit capabilities. By computing the Aggregate Score using Equation (4), organizational leadership could identify precise maturity gaps and allocate resources to the specific dimensions requiring prioritized intervention. Additionally, Equation (5) generalizes the model by allowing the number of dimensions to be expanded or reconfigured in line with the adopting organization’s strategic priorities and contextual requirements. This represents a managerial implication of the research, as it may enable organizations to identify critical implementation gaps, such as (1) strong AI integration in compliance but weak integration in governance; (2) strong data acquisition capabilities but weak internal audit intelligence; or (3) implementation concentrated at descriptive and diagnostic levels, with little predictive or prescriptive maturity. Together, the IA-AI Cube and HWSM may support the IAF in assessing the overall level of AI adoption and in identifying specific areas where AI capabilities remain experimental, partially implemented, or fully optimized. The IA-AIIF, IA-AI Cube, and HWSM could also provide actionable guidance for organizations that include AI in their IAF strategy. As such, organizations may consider a possible implementation pathway that includes (1) evaluating AI maturity using multidimensional models such as the IA-AI Cube and HWSM model; (2) targeting high levels of internal audit maturity, such as prescriptive and autonomous auditing; (3) establishing standard operating procedures (SOPs) to ensure consistent scoring for each dimension of the model.

### 6.3. Future Research Directions

The IA-AI Cube could be further developed by incorporating additional dimensions, such as ESG audit capabilities or cybersecurity risk assessment, thereby expanding its applicability across diverse organizational settings. Another avenue for future research involves the empirical validation of the IA-AIIF and the proposed maturity scoring model through testing in real-world internal audit environments. An additional research direction with direct practical relevance concerns the analytical competencies and emerging hybrid roles required to operationalize AI within the IAF. These may include roles such as an AI governance officer, AI risk and compliance officer, and AI integration architect. Subsequent studies could explore how such roles might be effectively integrated into existing internal audit structures while preserving the IAF’s functional independence and governance requirements.
